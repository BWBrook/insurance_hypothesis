# Shared environments can destabilise communities with balanced opposing responses

## Abstract

Species that respond in opposite directions to the same environmental fluctuation can offset one another's changes in abundance, so a shared environment is expected to stabilise communities with balanced opposing responses. I tested this expectation in stochastic Ricker communities of competing species. With small fluctuations it holds: near-perfect environmental correlation cut the mean squared coefficient of variation (CV²) of total abundance by 68% when responses were balanced, and tripled it when the same responses had aligned signs. The benefit of a shared environment declined as fluctuations grew. In richer communities it reversed, up to doubling CV², although responses remained perfectly balanced and species' fluctuations still partly offset one another. The reason is that compensation does not remove environmental variation; it moves it into community composition. Under a shared environment all species are displaced along the same compositional axis in the same years, and total abundance lies above equilibrium in steps in which that displacement grows and below it in steps in which it shrinks. A second-order calculation captures this effect; its predictions were within 4% of simulated ratios in 23 small-fluctuation settings specified before simulation. Its coefficient, multiplied by the environmental variance, also ordered the full nonlinear outcomes within each richness level. In a second prespecified test with new competition and autocorrelation values, interpolation along this axis predicted all 35 evaluable ratios within 5% and correctly called whether each of 17 evaluable eight-species settings would be buffered or destabilised. Reversals needed large fluctuations or persistent compositional departures, and appeared more readily in richer communities. Response diversity therefore does not guarantee that a shared environment adds stability: the outcome also depends on fluctuation size and on how long compositional departures persist.

## Keywords

biodiversity; response diversity; ecosystem stability; compensatory dynamics; environmental covariance; nonlinear population dynamics; insurance hypothesis.

## Introduction

When a warm year favours one species and harms another, their changes can offset one another in the community total. This compensation is a central mechanism of biodiversity insurance: species with different environmental responses can stabilise aggregate abundance, biomass or productivity [@doak1998StatisticalInevitabilityStabilitydiversity; @yachi1999BiodiversityEcosystemProductivity; @gonzalez2009CausesConsequencesCompensatory; @thibaut2013UnderstandingDiversityStability; @loreau2021BiodiversityInsuranceConcept]. Sharing an environmental fluctuation can strengthen that protection. The same warm year promotes one species and suppresses its opposite responder, whereas independent exposures need not produce gains and losses at the same time. If both species respond in the same direction, shared exposure instead makes their fluctuations reinforce one another. Whether a common environment stabilises a community therefore depends on the organisms that experience it.

The strength of compensation also depends on how much each species contributes. A rare cool-favoured species cannot fully offset a dominant warm-favoured one, even if their per-capita responses are equal and opposite. Stochastic community theory accounts for differences among species through response covariances and the feedbacks that transmit environmental shocks through population dynamics [@ives1999StabilityVariabilityCompetitive; @loreau2008SpeciesSynchronyIts; @loreau2013BiodiversityEcosystemStability]. Abundance weighting is also central to measuring response diversity [@ross2023HowMeasureResponse], and across natural and seminatural communities, stability tracks synchrony among the dominant species more closely than species richness [@valencia2020SynchronyMattersMore]. For small fluctuations around a stable equilibrium, these ideas give a clear prediction in a symmetric competitive community: shared exposure reduces total-abundance variability when opposing abundance-weighted responses cancel sufficiently, and perfect balance removes the shared environmental input from the linearised total.

How much of this protection survives when fluctuations are large? The question is practical as well as theoretical: temperature extremes are expected to become more frequent and severe, and changes in environmental variance can affect species more than changes in the mean [@vasseur2014IncreasedTemperatureVariation]. Population change depends on current abundance and density feedback, so cancellation of the direct environmental input need not produce cancellation of the resulting abundance changes. Even at a balanced equilibrium, multiplicative growth makes the gain under a favourable shock larger than the loss under an equally strong unfavourable shock. Away from equilibrium, the abundance of each responder and its subsequent density-dependent adjustment also matter. Existing theory explicitly recognises its small-perturbation assumption [@demazancourt2013PredictingEcosystemStability]. Here I ask how the benefit of shared exposure changes beyond that limit, and whether sharing an environment can make a perfectly balanced community more variable than independent exposure would.

Previous multispecies Ricker simulations have linked environmental covariance, temporal colour and community assembly to diversity–stability relationships [@fowler2013ColonizationCovarianceColour]. Work on response diversity likewise emphasises that stability depends on the responses and interactions of the constituent species [@mori2013ResponseDiversityDetermines; @ross2024LimitedTheoreticalEmpirical; @ross2026ExpertledPrioritiesResponse]. Testing the contribution of response direction requires a comparison that holds response magnitudes fixed. Testing the contribution of shared exposure requires a second comparison, between correlated and independent environments within the same community. These comparisons address different questions: opposing responses can remain advantageous relative to aligned responses even if environmental correlation no longer adds stability.

I use stochastic Ricker communities to connect these two comparisons to fluctuation size. A sign-only contrast first establishes how opposing responses alter the effect of exposure correlation. I then measure how that effect changes with the size of environmental fluctuations, derive its leading departure from the small-fluctuation limit, and test the calculation against simulations whose outcomes were predicted in advance. The central result is that a shared environment can make a perfectly balanced community more variable than independent environments would. Such a reversal cannot occur in the linearised theory, where perfect balance removes the shared input entirely, and to my knowledge it has not been reported. Compensation does not remove environmental variation; it moves it from total abundance into community composition. When all species experience the same fluctuation, they are all displaced along the same compositional axis in the same years, and total abundance departs from equilibrium according to whether that displacement has just grown or shrunk, whichever species are ahead. One coefficient from the calculation, multiplied by the environmental variance, provides the axis along which this effect overtakes the benefit of compensation.

## Methods

### Community dynamics and environmental exposure

The abundance of species $i$ follows a competitive stochastic Ricker map,
\[
N_i(t+1)=N_i(t)\exp\left[r_0+b_i z_i(t)-\frac{N_i(t)+\alpha\sum_{j\ne i}N_j(t)}{K_i}\right].
\]
Here $r_0$ is intrinsic growth, $K_i$ scales density dependence, intraspecific competition is fixed at one, and $\alpha$ is interspecific competition. Defaults are $r_0=1$, $K_i=100$ and $\alpha=0.3$. An isolated species has equilibrium abundance $r_0K_i$; with equal $K_i=K$, the community equilibrium is $N_i^\ast=n=r_0K/[1+\alpha(S-1)]$. For unequal $K_i$, the interior equilibrium solves $C_\alpha\mathbf N^\ast=r_0\mathbf K$, where $C_\alpha$ has diagonal entries one and off-diagonal entries $\alpha$.

The response slope $b_i$ determines whether growth increases or decreases with exposure $z_i$, and by how much. **Mixed** responses are evenly spaced in $[-0.5,0.5]$, so they sum to zero; odd-richness communities also contain a zero slope. **Matched aligned** responses use the same magnitudes with nonnegative signs, $b_i=|b_i^{\mathrm{mixed}}|$. Reversing signs under symmetric Gaussian exposures preserves each species' marginal forcing distribution and changes only the covariance of forcing among species. A **steep aligned** treatment, with slopes evenly spaced in $[0.1,1.0]$, is retained in the Supporting information; it changes magnitude as well as direction and is not a sign-only control.

Exposures follow a multivariate AR(1) process,
\[
\mathbf z_t=\phi\mathbf z_{t-1}+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_t,
\qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}^2C(\rho_{\mathrm{eff}})).
\]
The equicorrelation matrix $C$ has diagonal entries one and common off-diagonal correlation $\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}$. All exposures represent the same physically oriented variable: a positive anomaly means, for example, a warmer temperature for every species, although trajectories can differ among microhabitats. The innovation SD $\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}$ differs from the stationary exposure SD, $\sigma_z=\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}/\sqrt{1-\phi^2}$. Thus increasing $\phi$ at fixed innovation variance changes both persistence and stationary variance. Defaults use $\phi=0.4$.

For $S>1$, positive definiteness requires $-1/(S-1)<\rho<1$. Requested correlations are converted to
\[
\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}=\min\!\left(\max\!\left(\rho_{\mathrm{env}},-\frac1{S-1}+10^{-6}\right),1-10^{-6}\right).
\]
Both values are retained. I refer to $\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}=1-10^{-6}$ as near-perfect correlation; $\rho=0$ gives independent exposures. The lower bound also clips the requested $S=4$, $\rho_{\mathrm{env}}=-0.5$ treatment in the supplementary persistence gradient.

The main experiments use 1000 replicate trajectories per scenario, each of 600 time steps with the first 300 discarded. A robustness experiment uses longer trajectories, described below. Exposures start at zero and abundances at $K_i/2$. Seeds are deterministic functions of a grid's base seed, scenario and replicate indices; environmental realisations are not paired across treatments. An abundance floor of $N_{\min}=10^{-9}$ and exponent clipping to $[-50,50]$ prevent numerical failure. Floor contact and guard diagnostics are reported separately from ecological interpretation. Table 1 summarises the design; the complete grid inventory and floor occupancy are in the Supporting information.

### Stability comparisons and the linear benchmark

Community variability is the temporal coefficient of variation of total abundance, $N_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_i N_i$, computed separately for each retained trajectory using the sample mean and sample variance with divisor $L-1$, where $L=300$ in the main experiments and $L=3000$ in the longer runs. I compare mixed with matched aligned communities under the same environmental conditions to measure the sign-only advantage. To measure the additional effect of exposure correlation within a community, I use
\[
R=\frac{\mathbb E_{\mathrm{rep}}[\mathrm{CV}_{\mathrm{tot}}^2\mid\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}=1-10^{-6}]}{\mathbb E_{\mathrm{rep}}[\mathrm{CV}_{\mathrm{tot}}^2\mid\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}=0]}.
\]
This is a ratio of mean replicate squared CVs. Values below one indicate buffering by shared exposure; values above one indicate amplification. These terms describe a relative comparison, not an absolute decline in variability as environmental variance increases. Direct matched-to-mixed comparisons at shared exposure use their own mean squared CVs, rather than the quotient of two separately estimated values of $R$.

For small fluctuations, species $i$ contributes $a_i=N_i^\ast b_i$ to the aggregate environmental input $F=\sum_i a_i z_i$. Under equicorrelation,
\[
\operatorname{Var}(F)=\sigma_z^2\left[(1-\rho_{\mathrm{eff}})\sum_i a_i^2+\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(\sum_i a_i\right)^2\right].
\]
The abundance-weighted alignment index
\[
A=\frac{(\sum_i a_i)^2}{S\sum_i a_i^2}
\]
ranges from zero for balanced weighted responses to one for identical weighted responses. Increasing exposure correlation reduces forcing variance when $A<1/S$ and increases it when $A>1/S$. Relative to independent exposure, the forcing-variance ratio is $(1-\rho_{\mathrm{eff}})+\rho_{\mathrm{eff}}SA$.

With equal $K_i$, total abundance near equilibrium follows a single linear mode, $X_{t+1}=\lambda_cX_t+F_t$, where $\lambda_c=1-r_0$. Because every exposure has the same temporal autocorrelation, the stationary variance multiplier from this feedback is independent of exposure correlation and cancels in the ratio. The forcing benchmark therefore also gives the ratio of stationary total-abundance variances in the linearised equal-$K$ model. With unequal $K_i$, both the weights and the feedback matrix change, so the forcing benchmark alone no longer determines abundance variability. Nonlinear dynamics can break the correspondence even with equal weights (Supporting information).

### A small-fluctuation prediction for balanced communities

Perfectly shared exposure and balanced slopes remove the first-order total-abundance response. They do not remove departures of individual populations from equilibrium. In the equal-$K$ model, these opposing departures persist through the contrast mode,
\[
\lambda_d=1-r_0\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha(S-1)}.
\]
This eigenvalue is the fraction of a compositional departure, one that leaves total abundance unchanged, that remains after one step. It approaches one as interspecific competition approaches intraspecific competition or as species are added. The common-mode eigenvalue $\lambda_c$ plays the same role for departures of the total. Writing first- and second-order abundance departures as $\mathbf x^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf x^{(2)}$, with $X^{(2)}=\sum_i x_i^{(2)}$, expansion of the full Ricker update gives
\[
X_{t+1}^{(2)}=\lambda_cX_t^{(2)}+
\frac{\lVert\mathbf x_{t+1}^{(1)}\rVert^2-\lVert\mathbf x_t^{(1)}\rVert^2}{2n}.
\]
At $r_0=1$, where $\lambda_c=0$, the identity has a direct reading: total abundance lies above equilibrium after a step in which the summed squared departure of species from equilibrium has grown, and below it after a step in which that departure has shrunk. The second term is a signed change in squared departure, with zero stationary mean and nonzero variance. It includes abundance–growth products as well as curvature of the exponential growth response; it is not a uniformly positive convexity correction.

For $r_0=1$, stationary Gaussian forcing and exact shared exposure, the resulting population-variance ratio has leading form
\[
R_{\mathrm{pop}}=c\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}^2+O(\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}^4),
\qquad
c=\frac{\sum_i b_i^2}{(1-\lambda_d^2)(1-\lambda_d\phi)^2}.
\]
This local prediction assumes a stable positive equilibrium and inactive numerical guards. It applies to the balanced shared-exposure endpoint, not to an arbitrary partially aligned community. For the focal four-species community, $c=1.65465$. The expected 300-step sample-variance correction gives a leading coefficient of 1.66752 for the sampled CV ratio, a difference of 0.78%; imperfect correlation also leaves approximately $10^{-6}$ of the first-order ratio. The full derivation and finite-window calculation are in the Supporting information.

### Simulation contrasts and prospective tests

The focal sign-only experiment compares mixed and matched aligned responses at $S=4$ and 8, $\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}=0.5$, $\phi=0.4$, and requested correlations from zero to one in increments of 0.1 (Fig. 1A). A composition experiment at $S=4$ follows two paths between buffering and amplification (Fig. 1B). The first rotates the mixed response vector into its matched aligned counterpart,
\[
\mathbf b(q)=\cos(\pi q/2)\,\mathbf b_{\mathrm{mixed}}+
\sin(\pi q/2)\,|\mathbf b_{\mathrm{mixed}}|,
\]
for nine equally spaced $q\in[0,1]$. The endpoint vectors are orthogonal and have equal norm, so the rotation holds overall response magnitude fixed. The second retains the mixed slopes and varies $\mathbf K(d)=100(1-0.3d,1-0.1d,1+0.1d,1+0.3d)$ for $d\in\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1,1.1\}$, using the balanced endpoint for $d=0$. Every treatment has a positive interior equilibrium, and the constant sum of $K_i$ holds deterministic total abundance constant. This path changes feedback as well as abundance weights. An endpoint sensitivity repeats the sign contrast at $r_0\in\{0.25,0.5,1,1.5\}$ (Fig. 1C).

To measure amplitude dependence, a new grid repeats the 14 composition treatments at $\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7,1,1.3\}$, with independent and near-perfectly shared exposures (Fig. 2). The balanced and matched aligned endpoints supply the sign-only amplitude comparison. Older grids independently replicate the balanced amplitude curve and extend it into a floor-affected stress region. Boundary comparisons use the simulated treatment points and their uncertainty. Where crossings are linearly interpolated between adjacent treatments, they are descriptive summaries; the design does not resolve an exact continuous threshold.

The second-order prediction was derived from the focal setting before three additional grids crossed $\alpha\in\{0.1,0.3,0.6\}$, $S\in\{2,4,8\}$ and $\phi\in\{0,0.4,0.8\}$, with mixed responses, $r_0=1$, equal $K_i$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}\in\{0.1,0.2,0.5,1\}$, and both correlation endpoints (Fig. 3). Predictions were calculated for all 27 parameter combinations before these simulations. One combination repeats the derivation setting with independent seeds; the other 26 extend it. The prespecified small-fluctuation subset comprises combinations with predicted $R<0.1$ at $\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}=0.1$. Success required the simulated ratio to lie within 20% of the prediction and the ratio $R(0.2)/R(0.1)$ to lie between three and five. Two recorded versions of the scaling criterion differ in one eligibility restriction, and both were evaluated (Supporting information). Larger predicted departures test the limits of the approximation, and no floor-affected cell is discarded.

Supplementary grids retain the realised-synchrony comparison, the steep aligned stress extension, a deterministic richness assembly sequence and a fixed-innovation persistence gradient (Supporting information). Adding species in the assembly sequence changes slope composition, root-mean-square slope and equilibrium abundance together. Increasing $\phi$ at fixed innovation SD changes persistence and stationary variance together. Neither sequence identifies a pure effect of richness or environmental persistence.

Five balanced-response settings in which shared exposure increased variability without retained floor contact were then repeated with 6000 steps, 3000 burn-in and 500 independently seeded replicates per exposure condition. This follow-up was specified before the longer runs, with success defined as a ratio above one and a 95% Monte Carlo lower bound also above one in each of the five settings. The original parameter settings were retained without expansion.

Within each richness level, the ratios in the three mechanism grids turned out to be rank-ordered by $x=c\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}^2$ well beyond the small-fluctuation regime (Results). Because this pattern was noticed after the simulations, I tested it with two further grids whose predictions were recorded before they ran. These used competition and autocorrelation values absent from the earlier grids, $\alpha\in\{0.2,0.45\}$ and $\phi\in\{0.2,0.6\}$, crossed with $S\in\{4,8,12\}$, innovation SD 0.2, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7 and 1, and both correlation endpoints. For $S=4$ and 8, each prediction interpolated $\log R$ linearly in $\log x$ between the floor-free cells of the earlier grids with the same richness, without extrapolation; the predictions therefore test $x$ as an axis calibrated on earlier simulations, not the leading-order formula itself. The three criteria, fixed in advance, concerned accuracy, the side of one on which eight-species ratios fell, and whether twelve-species ratios lay above the eight-species curve (Supporting information).

To show the mechanism directly, trajectories of 3300 steps, with the first 300 discarded, were simulated for four balanced settings under each exposure condition: one for display and 20 for summary statistics. Total-abundance departures were compared with the second-order driver computed from the simulated species departures.

### Uncertainty and diagnostic checks

Ninety-five per cent Monte Carlo intervals for $R$ use a log-ratio delta method applied to the replicate squared CV distributions. They quantify simulation error within the model, not uncertainty about its structure. An additional multiplicity check used Bonferroni-adjusted 95% familywise intervals across all 108 setting-by-amplitude comparisons in the mechanism grids, and across the 60 comparisons of the ordering test. Means of CV and ratios of mean squared CV are kept distinct. Production summaries also retain the exact variance decomposition, $\operatorname{Var}(N_{\mathrm{tot}})=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(N_i)+2\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(N_i,N_j)$. A negative aggregate covariance indicates that population fluctuations partly offset one another; it is descriptive accounting, not an independent causal test.

Abundance synchrony and mean pairwise log-growth correlation provide further diagnostics in the Supporting information. The implementation defines log-growth using $\log[N_i(t+1)+N_{\min}]-\log[N_i(t)+N_{\min}]$, adding an offset after abundance flooring. Zero-forcing synchrony is undefined. Floor occupancy is the fraction of retained steps at $N_{\min}$, averaged over species; floored populations can recover, so this is not biological extinction. A separate guard diagnostic examines full trajectories, including burn-in, at all five balanced-reversal settings with 200 full 600-step and 100 full 6000-step trajectories per exposure condition, seeded separately from the production runs. Its coverage is distinct from the retained production summaries and from the earlier three-scenario stress diagnostic (Supporting information).

## Results

### Response signs determine whether shared exposure helps

Under independent exposures the mixed and matched aligned treatments have the same forcing distribution and nearly identical variability. At the focal $S=4$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}=0.5$ setting, both have mean $\mathrm{CV}_{\mathrm{tot}}=0.106$. At exposure correlation 0.5 the mixed and matched aligned means separate to 0.084 and 0.149, and near perfect correlation they are 0.059 and 0.184 (Fig. 1A). The corresponding shared-to-independent squared-CV ratios are 0.320 (95% Monte Carlo interval 0.315–0.325) and 2.99 (2.97–3.02). Changing response signs alone therefore reverses the effect of exposure correlation.

The response rotation passes from buffering to amplification, but nonlinear dynamics displace the transition from its first-order benchmark (Fig. 1B). At the tested alignment $A=0.247$, the benchmark predicts weak buffering ($R=0.988$), whereas the simulation shows weak amplification ($R=1.043$, 1.028–1.059). The balanced endpoint retains substantial variability where the benchmark predicts almost none. Along the unequal-$K_i$ path, $R$ changes from 0.964 (0.952–0.975) at $A=0.230$ to 1.053 (1.041–1.065) at $A=0.257$. Here response signs are unchanged: altered abundance weights and feedback together remove the benefit of shared exposure. The benchmark at the upper point is 1.030, so nonlinear departures do not universally compress ratios towards one.

The sign contrast survives all four tested growth rates (Fig. 1C). At $r_0=0.25,0.5,1$ and 1.5, mixed-response ratios are 0.663, 0.439, 0.320 and 0.323; matched aligned ratios are 2.77, 2.94, 2.99 and 2.97. The result is therefore not confined to $r_0=1$, where the linearised total-abundance mode has no direct carryover from the previous step.

### Larger fluctuations weaken the benefit and change the composition boundary

In the matched amplitude grid, the balanced community's shared-to-independent ratio rises from approximately 0.017 at innovation SD 0.1 to 0.320 at 0.5 and 0.961 at 1.3 (Fig. 2A). The older independent-seed grid gives corresponding values of 0.0165, 0.3197 and 0.9371. The benefit of correlated exposure thus declines strongly as fluctuations grow. With the same competition and autocorrelation, richness changes how fast the benefit fades. The balanced eight-species ratio rises from 0.045 at SD 0.1 to 0.77 at SD 0.5 and 1.55 (1.52–1.58) at SD 1.0, so sharing the environment has become destabilising, whereas the two-species ratio reaches only 0.55. Matched aligned communities remain amplified, although their ratio falls from 3.19 to 2.31 across the four-species amplitude range. Directly comparing the two communities under shared exposure, aligned responses produce approximately 190 times the mean squared CV of balanced responses at SD 0.1 and 2.4 times at SD 1.3. Opposing signs retain an advantage even where the additional benefit of exposure correlation is small.

Amplitude also changes which compositions are buffered (Fig. 2B). Larger fluctuations flatten the whole rotation curve towards one: shared exposure buffers balanced compositions less and amplifies aligned compositions less. Compositions near the first-order boundary therefore change side. At alignment $A=0.247$, the ratio rises from 0.986 (0.978–0.995) at SD 0.1 to 1.056 (1.034–1.077) at SD 0.7, with no retained floor contact at either amplitude, so the same composition switches from buffering to amplification as fluctuations grow. Interpolating between neighbouring treatments places the rotation crossing near $A=0.25$ at SD 0.1 and $A=0.15$ at SD 1.3. Coarse spacing, an interval overlapping one and rare floor contact at SD 1.3 mean that these values summarise the shift rather than estimate a threshold (Supporting information). The unequal-$K_i$ path shifts in the same direction, with floor contact at its larger amplitudes.

### The onset of lost buffering is predictable

Twenty-three of the 27 parameter combinations meet the prespecified small-fluctuation eligibility criterion, including the independent-seed repeat of the derivation setting. All 23 pass both versions of the acceptance criteria (Fig. 3C; Table S4). At innovation SD 0.1, simulated ratios are 0.965–1.015 times the second-order prediction, across coefficients $c$ from 0.52 to 7.14, and doubling innovation SD raises the ratio by factors of 3.50–3.98, close to the predicted factor of four. The calculation therefore predicts the initial loss of buffering in 22 new parameter combinations as well as in the focal one. It over-predicts once the ratio is no longer small: in the focal community at SD 0.5 it gives 0.414, against a simulated 0.320.

### Total abundance follows the swing in composition

Simulated trajectories show the mechanism at work (Fig. 3A, B). Under shared exposure, the species of a balanced eight-species community are displaced in mirror image: species favoured by high exposure rise while those favoured by low exposure fall, and the composition swings back and forth along one axis. Because growth is multiplicative, the favoured species' gains can be much larger than the disfavoured species' losses. Total abundance follows the change in the summed squared species departure. Across 20 trajectories of 3000 retained steps, the mean correlation between the two is 1.00 in the focal community at innovation SD 0.1 and 0.93 at SD 0.5; in the eight-species setting of Fig. 3A it is 1.00 at SD 0.1 and 0.77 at SD 0.5.

Why the total follows the change in that displacement, rather than its size, is clearest for two species with equal and opposite responses. Suppose one is $d$ individuals above equilibrium and the other $d$ below, and a shared anomaly changes both by the same per-capita amount in opposite directions. The abundant species applies its change to a larger base than its depleted partner. If the anomaly widens the displacement, the abundant species gains more individuals than the depleted one loses, and the total rises. If it narrows the displacement, the abundant species loses more than the depleted one regains, and the total falls. Curvature of the exponential growth term adds a further gain whenever species move. Together these terms make the total departure equal to the change in summed squared departure divided by $2n$, which is the identity used above at $r_0=1$. In a shared environment, all species swing together, so these contributions add. Under independent exposure, species reach their largest departures in different years. At SD 0.1 the driver is then less variable by a factor of 1.74 in the focal community and 2.12 in the eight-species setting, and the total instead follows the first-order input that independent exposures leave uncancelled (mean correlation with the driver 0.11 and 0.26). At SD 0.5 the factors narrow to 1.33 and 1.15.

### Shared exposure can destabilise a perfectly balanced community

Five settings of the mechanism grids have balanced responses, no retained floor contact at either exposure endpoint, and a shared-to-independent ratio whose interval lies entirely above one (Fig. 3C; Table S5). At $S=8$, $\alpha=0.6$, $\phi=0.4$ and innovation SD 0.5, the ratio is 1.458 (1.429–1.488): sharing the environment increases mean squared community CV by about 46%. Three other eight-species settings at SD 1.0 give ratios from 1.19 to 1.65, and a fifth, at $\alpha=0.6$, $\phi=0.8$ and SD 0.2, gives 1.465 (1.430–1.501). All five Bonferroni-adjusted familywise intervals across the 108 mechanism-grid comparisons remain above one.

All five reversals persisted in the longer trajectories, with ratios from 1.187 to 1.654 and every lower interval bound above 1.17. The SD 0.5 example gave 1.457 (1.444–1.470), almost unchanged from the original estimate. Four settings remained free of retained floor contact; in the fifth, one replicate had a floor frequency of 0.00033. The separate full-trajectory diagnostic observed no exponent clipping or floor events at either trajectory length in any of the five settings (Supporting information).

Compensatory fluctuations persist during the reversal. In the SD 0.5 example, the aggregate covariance contribution is approximately −879 under independent exposure and −716 under shared exposure, while mean total-abundance variance increases from 144 to 210. The covariance remains negative but offsets less of the summed species variance under shared exposure. The reversal concerns the effect of environmental correlation on the whole community, not the disappearance of compensation.

### The small-noise coefficient predicts where reversals occur

The coefficient that predicts the onset also orders the outcomes far beyond it. Within each richness level of the mechanism grids, the simulated ratio rises almost monotonically with $x=c\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}^2$ across all three competition strengths, autocorrelations and amplitudes, with rank correlations of at least 0.999 among floor-free settings (Fig. 3C). Different combinations of fluctuation size, competition and environmental persistence that share a value of $x$ give nearly the same ratio. In eight-species communities, the ratio exceeds one in every floor-free setting with $x\ge1.67$ and lies below one in every setting with $x\le1.45$; the smallest $x$ with an interval entirely above one is 1.85. Beyond the small-noise regime, ratios at a given $x$ rise with richness from four species upward, and two- and four-species communities never exceeded one without floor contact.

All three criteria of the prospective test were met. Every one of the 35 evaluable floor-free four- and eight-species settings fell within 10% of its prediction, with a largest error of 4.6%. Without the axis, predictions were poor: the small-noise line $R=x$ came within 10% in only 8 of these settings, and interpolation in fluctuation size alone, ignoring competition and autocorrelation, in 6. All 17 floor-free eight-species settings fell on the predicted side of one, and all six predicted reversals among them had 95% intervals above one. Two of these used $\alpha=0.2$, weaker competition than the default: at $\phi=0.2$ and SD 1.0 the predicted ratio was 1.155 and the simulated ratio 1.150 (1.131–1.170). Precision held close to the boundary: at $\alpha=0.45$, $\phi=0.6$ and SD 0.35, the prediction was 1.074 and the simulation 1.077 (1.057–1.097). Fourteen of 15 floor-free twelve-species settings lay above the eight-species curve; the exception was at the smallest $x$, where all richness levels share the small-noise line. Seven twelve-species settings reversed without floor contact. The largest floor-free ratio, 2.03 (1.99–2.07) at $\alpha=0.45$, $\phi=0.2$ and SD 0.7, means that sharing the environment doubled mean squared community CV despite perfectly balanced responses. All 13 floor-free reversals in the test grids remain above one after Bonferroni adjustment across its 60 comparisons.

The richness effect is relative. From four to twelve species, mean squared CV under independent exposure fell to 0.24–0.30 of its four-species value in every floor-free comparison of the test grids, whereas under shared exposure it ranged from 0.87 to 1.56 times that value.

## Discussion

A community can contain perfectly balanced opposing responders and still become more variable when its members share the same environmental fluctuations. Environmental correlation then changes from a source of buffering into a source of amplification, with no change in the sign or balance of responses. In the richest communities tested, sharing the environment doubled the mean squared CV of total abundance. The matched-sign control shows why shared exposure helps, the amplitude experiments show how that help fades, and a second-order calculation, tested twice against predictions made before simulation, predicts when buffering begins to erode and supplies the axis along which it is lost.

The key is that balanced responses do not make total abundance constant. Compensation cancels the direct environmental effects in the total by displacing species in opposite directions. Changes in the size of that compositional displacement then reach the total through multiplicative growth and density feedback, whichever species are ahead. A shared environment makes these contributions coherent: all species are displaced along the same axis in the same years, so their squared departures rise and fall together, whereas under independent exposure they partly average out. The quadratic input has zero mean and can take either sign. The one-step convexity of multiplicative growth is only part of it; abundance–growth products and density feedback are needed to obtain the stationary result.

The coefficient $c$ combines the two ingredients of this effect: the combined response magnitude, $\sum_i b_i^2$, and the persistence of compositional departures through $\lambda_d$ and environmental autocorrelation. Because the outcome depends mainly on their product with the environmental variance, $x=c\sigma_{\mathrm{env}}^2$, a community of the same richness with weak competition and large fluctuations can have nearly the same ratio as one with strong competition and modest fluctuations. The leading-order magnitude is wrong at large $x$, but $x$ still ordered the nonlinear outcomes, and interpolation along it, calibrated on earlier simulations, predicted ratios and reversals in new parameter combinations. The calculation therefore identifies the main axis of the process even where the expansion that produced it fails. It does not capture richness: at the same $x$, richer communities had higher ratios. That effect is relative. Adding species reduced variability under independent exposure, as portfolio theory predicts [@doak1998StatisticalInevitabilityStabilitydiversity; @thibaut2013UnderstandingDiversityStability], but did little under shared exposure, where the coherent compositional swing does not average out. Sharing the environment therefore cost more, relative to independence, in richer communities. In the tested sequence, adding species also adds response slopes and lowers equilibrium abundance, so this is not an isolated effect of richness. A parallel conclusion has been reached for pulse disturbances, where response diversity predicted community stability only in the absence of interspecific interactions [@kunze2026SpeciesInteractionsDetermine]. In both cases, response diversity alone did not determine stability.

How unusual are the conditions for reversal? In this model they are not extreme. In the twelve-species community with $\alpha=0.45$ and $\phi=0.6$, an innovation SD of 0.35 nearly doubled mean squared community CV ($R=1.89$). The stationary SD of the environmental effect on the log growth of the most responsive species is then 0.22, so a one-SD environmental anomaly multiplies its per-capita growth by about 1.25 or 0.80. In eight-species communities with $\alpha=0.6$ and $\phi=0.8$, an SD of 0.17 on the same scale was enough ($R=1.47$). These competition coefficients are strong relative to the four- to five-fold excess of intraspecific over interspecific competition typical of plant communities [@adler2018CompetitionCoexistencePlant], but reversals also occurred at $\alpha=0.2$ once that SD reached 0.44 with eight species or 0.31 with twelve. Positive autocorrelation, which prolongs compositional departures, is common in environmental time series [@vasseur2004ColorEnvironmentalNoise]. Individual populations swing widely in these settings (Fig. 3A); with demographic stochasticity, their troughs would carry an extinction risk that this model does not represent. Whether natural communities reach these conditions depends on parameters the model does not estimate. The model does show that reversal is not confined to an isolated corner of parameter space: it occupies a predictable region defined by $x$ and richness.

Opposing responses still reduce variability relative to aligned ones. Across the matched four-species amplitude series, balanced communities are less variable than aligned communities under shared exposure, even when sharing the environment provides little additional protection. The distinction is between the benefit of response direction and the benefit of environmental correlation within a community. Empirical surveys find compensatory dynamics less often than synchronous ones [@houlahan2007CompensatoryDynamicsAre]; the present results add that, where compensation does occur, it is not evidence that a shared environment stabilises the community. A negative aggregate covariance or low synchrony does not establish that increasing exposure correlation will stabilise it. Negative covariance persists in the reversal settings because some population fluctuations still offset one another. Covariance-based synchrony metrics describe that accounting, but they do not isolate the process that generated it [@loreau2008SpeciesSynchronyIts; @loreau2013BiodiversityEcosystemStability].

The composition boundary makes the same limitation visible away from perfect balance. Increasing the alignment of responses changes correlated exposure from buffering to amplification, and increasing fluctuation size moves that transition towards less aligned compositions. Changing equilibrium abundances while retaining every response sign can also remove buffering. Because the unequal-$K_i$ path changes feedback as well as weights, its results should be interpreted jointly. For empirical work, a species list or an unweighted tally of response signs is insufficient. Responses must be weighted by contributions to the aggregate and interpreted alongside the environmental and population dynamics [@ross2023HowMeasureResponse; @polazzo2024MeasuringResponseDiversity].

The mechanism leaves a signature that can be sought in community time series. Where species respond in opposite directions to a shared driver, total abundance should be higher after intervals in which the summed squared departures of species from their typical abundances have grown, and lower after intervals in which those departures have shrunk. In the model this holds step by step at $r_0=1$; at other growth rates, earlier changes also contribute, with weights that decay by $\lambda_c$ per step. More generally, where direct environmental responses nearly balance, the relative benefit of shared exposure should decline as per-capita shocks grow, especially where compositional departures persist. If environmental variability increases as projected [@vasseur2014IncreasedTemperatureVariation], communities that now gain stability from shared environments may gain less. Testing these predictions requires consistently oriented environmental measurements, species response functions, contribution weights and information about population feedback. The orientation matters: if each species has its own freely reversible latent environmental axis, response signs and exposure correlations cannot be interpreted separately. The invariant quantity is then the covariance of demographic shocks, $D_b\Sigma_zD_b$, where $D_b=\operatorname{diag}(b_i)$. With several physical drivers, a community may compensate along one axis and respond in the same direction along another; the full covariance and response structure must be retained [@ross2023HowMeasureResponse; @polazzo2024MeasuringResponseDiversity].

These findings extend established theory within a deliberately restricted model. Earlier work already links abundance weighting, environmental covariance and dynamical feedback, and explicitly acknowledges the small-perturbation assumption [@ives1999StabilityVariabilityCompetitive; @demazancourt2013PredictingEcosystemStability]. Earlier Ricker simulations also show that environmental covariance and temporal colour interact with community assembly [@fowler2013ColonizationCovarianceColour]. Nonlinear averaging is usually invoked to explain how variability shifts mean performance [@ruel1999JensensInequalityPredicts]; the second-order input here has zero mean and instead transfers variance from community composition to the total. The prespecified tests check the calculation against the simulation model, not against field data. Here the environmental response is linear in log growth, competition is symmetric, and exact response-magnitude controls separate sign effects. Demographic stochasticity, observation error, dispersal and trophic interactions are absent. Different density dependence, nonlinear environmental response curves and asymmetric interactions can alter the second-order contribution and its stability consequences. The derived coefficient and observed reversal should therefore be tested under those alternatives before being treated as general properties of biodiversity insurance.

The supplementary assembly and persistence gradients have a narrower role. Their changing slope compositions, equilibrium abundances and stationary environmental variances prevent isolated causal claims about richness or temporal colour. Their severe-forcing regions also contact the numerical floor. The main reversal persists across longer observation windows, and the numerical diagnostics support its interpretation as a property of the population dynamics. These checks do not estimate its frequency in natural communities. Stability here is temporal variability of total abundance; resistance, recovery and multifunctional stability are different properties [@ives2007StabilityDiversityEcosystems; @mori2013ResponseDiversityDetermines; @loreau2021BiodiversityInsuranceConcept].

Balanced response diversity can therefore coexist with a destabilising effect of shared environmental variation. The variation that compensation removes from total abundance remains in community composition, and nonlinear population growth can return it to the total. How much returns depends on the size of fluctuations, on how long compositional departures persist and on richness, not only on the balance of species' responses. A coefficient from small-fluctuation theory, scaled by the environmental variance and calibrated for richness, locates where shared exposure turns from buffering to amplification.
