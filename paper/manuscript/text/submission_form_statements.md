## Significance Statement

Species with opposing environmental responses can compensate, but perfect response balance does not guarantee that sharing an environment stabilises their community. In stochastic Ricker communities, shared exposure sometimes increases total-abundance variability even while species fluctuations continue to offset one another. A second-order calculation predicts the initial loss of buffering across prespecified community settings; simulations establish that the benefit can reverse as fluctuations grow. The distinction matters for interpreting response diversity: opposing responses can remain beneficial relative to aligned responses even when environmental correlation adds variability. Predicting stability therefore requires fluctuation size and population feedback alongside response balance and species contributions.
